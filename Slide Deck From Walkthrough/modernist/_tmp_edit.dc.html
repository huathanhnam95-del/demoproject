Month 1 reflection 03 / 18

Hurdle 1: The Habit of Waiting for Permission

The thought

Who is going to tell me what to do next? Can I just rewrite this dialogue, or do I need someone to sign off on it?

The shift

In a traditional job the coordinator hands you the syllabus, the schedule and the format. Here that authority simply isn't there, and the first week of silence feels like a delay.

It isn't. If you see something that will help our students learn better, you already have permission. Find a colleague and start.

Heard in week 1

"I sat down on the Monday and waited for a syllabus that was never coming."
Month 1 reflection 04 / 18

Hurdle 2: Wondering If We're Doing Enough

The thought

Did I do enough today? Does anyone know how hard I worked?

The shift

Phantom guilt fills the space praise and deadlines used to occupy. Some of us answered messages at midnight to prove our dedication.

Working late does not make you a hero. Good pronunciation material comes from clear, well-rested minds. Trust that your work speaks for itself.

Heard in week 3

"Nobody asked me for hours, so I kept working to be safe."
Month 1 reflection 05 / 18

Hurdle 3: Holding Back to Be Polite

The thought

This draft confuses me — but I don't want to hurt anyone's feelings or sound critical.

The shift

Polite silence leaves a teammate alone with a weak lesson — and the confusion lands on a student weeks later.

Kindness is honest feedback, early: warm about the person, straight about the draft. We critique the work together so the class gets our best.

Heard in week 4

"I noticed the problem on day one. I said it on day nine."

Welcome to Flatland: How Valve Works Without Bosses

01

Zero managers

02

Desks on wheels

03

Learner first

Foundational philosophy 07 / 18

Gabe Newell's Ideology: Why Hierarchies Destroy Creative Value

"If you hire smart, creative people and then tell them what to do, you destroy most of their value. You want people who figure out what is valuable and just go do it."

Why pyramids existed

Ronald Coase, 1937: hierarchy was the cheapest way to coordinate repetitive factory work.

Why it breaks creative work

Lesson design is not repetitive. The same layers add friction, delay and distortion instead of order.

Nobody to manage up to

With no supervisor to impress, the whole team's attention points outward — at the learner.

Practical teamwork 09 / 18

Three Great Teamwork Habits We Learn from Valve

01

See a need, take the lead

No committee meeting required to start improving a lesson.

In practice: the minimal-pair drill nobody owned got rewritten on a Tuesday by whoever noticed.

02

Build live together

Thirty minutes on a shared doc beats five emails over three days.

In practice: draft the dialogue with the phonetics checker already in the room.

03

Let learning settle debates

Two approaches, no agreement? Test both with five students.

In practice: real results end the argument faster than an hour of debate.

Team pitfalls 10 / 18

Three Traps to Watch Out For

After Jo Freeman

Unwritten rules and cliques

When formal rules disappear, informal popularity can quietly take their place. We keep every draft open, visible and welcoming.

The consensus trap

Endless talking loops

All twelve of us do not need to agree on every sentence. Test the idea with real learners instead of debating it into paralysis.

After Rich Geldreich

Quiet overtime

Nobody should feel pressure to answer messages after hours, and nobody earns credit for a late-night reply.

When your day is done, log off and rest. The lamp goes out with you.

+ 4 days

Teamwork in practice 12 / 18

How We Work Better: Small Working Hubs

Three teachers, one shared document, ninety minutes. One drafts the dialogue, one checks the phonetics, one reads it aloud for rhythm.

Instant feedback

Seconds, not weeks — the question gets answered by the person sitting next to you.

Collective pride

Nobody hands over a draft and hopes. The lesson belongs to the room.

Shared energy

Written, polished and ready to test before lunch.

Making decisions as equals 14 / 18

How We Make Decisions: The Advice Process

01

You spot the call

Anyone can make any operational decision. Noticing it makes it yours.

02

Ask the experienced

Talk to teammates who know that area — phonetics, recording, assessment.

03

Ask the affected

Talk to whoever will teach, record or mark the thing you are changing. Listen properly.

04

Decide and own it

Make the call, explain your reasons, carry the result.

The advice process as Dennis Bakke ran it and Frédéric Laloux documented it: seek advice widely, then decide alone.

Advice is required. Agreement is not. No votes, no vetoes.

Working through differences 15 / 18

When We Disagree: How We Choose the Best Way

No long debates where people wear each other down. Three pathways, in this order.

Pathway one

The advice call

The person doing the work makes the final call, after hearing the people with experience and the people affected.

Pathway two

The student pilot

If both ideas have merit, run both with a handful of real learners. Student clarity decides, not seniority.

Pathway three

A friendly third peer

If it feels stuck or personal, ask a trusted colleague to sit in on a fifteen-minute coffee chat so everyone is heard.

Complain in side channels

Concerns go to the person, not around them.

Perform late nights

Caring about excellence 17 / 18

Three Simple Ways We Check Our Work

One colleague. No context. Press play.

Check 01

The fresh ear test

Someone who wasn't in the writing room listens to the audio cold and tells you what they actually heard.

Check 02

The live phone test

Play the sample back on an ordinary phone speaker. If the vowel survives that, it survives a classroom.

Check 03

The five-student pilot

Run it with five learners and watch their faces. Say nothing. Their confusion is the most honest note you'll get.
