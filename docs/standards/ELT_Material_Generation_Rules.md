# ELT Material Generation Rules & Slide Transcripts

## Part 1: Original Slide Transcripts

---

### Slide 1: When using AI
- **Headline**: The main unit of materials development should not be the activity. It should be the sequence of learning experiences across time.
- Try to avoid doing one-off activities.
- Plan the AI across a series of texts.

---

### Slide 2: The Balance Principle
- We distinguish language-focused learning from meaning-focused input and output.
- AI-assisted materials should not reproduce the imbalance of many conventional courses, where almost all activity concerns explanation, controlled practice and testing.
- **A balanced set of materials should provide**:
  - **Language-focused learning**: Help learners notice and understand useful forms.
  - **Meaning-focused input**: Allow learners to understand spoken and written language.
  - **Meaning-focused output**: Allow learners to communicate meanings that matter.
  - **Fluency development**: Help learners process familiar language more efficiently.

---

### Slide 3: The Sequencing Principle
- The most important contribution AI can make is not generating more material. It is helping the teacher create continuity.
- **A principled sequence would move through**:
  1. **Initial encounter**: Learners meet useful language in accessible input.
  2. **Supported processing**: Glosses, visuals, or teacher guidance assist comprehension.
  3. **Repeated encounter**: The language reappears in a different text or situation.
  4. **Retrieval**: Learners recall meanings, forms, or content.
  5. **Meaningful use**: Learners use the language to communicate.
  6. **Fluency**: Learners process familiar language with greater speed.
  7. **Later recycling**: The language returns in later lessons/units.

---

### Slide 4: The Human Control Principle
- AI should be treated as a materials assistant rather than a pedagogical decision maker.
- **The teacher remains responsible for determining**:
  - **Learning purpose**: AI cannot infer the real curriculum from a vague request.
  - **Learner level**: AI labels such as A2 or B1 are not consistently reliable.
  - **Target language**: AI may select rare or marginal vocabulary.
  - **Cultural appropriateness**: Generated situations may include stereotypes or unsuitable assumptions.
  - **Factual accuracy**: Plausible language is not necessarily accurate language.
  - **Recycling**: AI usually treats each prompt as an isolated task unless instructed otherwise.
  - **Final quality**: Generated materials require editing, trialing, and professional judgement.

---

### Slide 5: Controlling the AI (Attention Principles)
- The AI should be instructed to design materials around **focus of attention**, **quantity of attention**, and **quality of attention**.
- AI should **not** merely generate an attractive activity.
- AI should create conditions in which learners attend to useful language, meet it often enough, and process it in ways that strengthen learning.
- AI must ensure that learners receive **enough exposure and practice**. One example, one worksheet, or one lesson is unlikely to produce durable learning.
- AI must make **each encounter contribute something new**. Repetition should strengthen meaning, form, use, associations, and accessibility.

---

### Slide 6: Controlling the AI (Example Prompts)
- **Example FOCUS prompt**: *"Keep the learning focus narrow and explicit. Do not introduce language or task demands that distract learners from the intended objective. Select only frequent, useful and level-appropriate target items."*
- **Example QUANTITY prompt**: *"Recycle each target item several times across the sequence. Reintroduce the items in different contexts and include later retrieval. Do not assume that one presentation and one practice activity will result in learning."*
- **Example QUALITY prompt**: *"Recycle target language through varied and meaningful encounters. Require learners to understand, retrieve, connect and use the language. Avoid mechanical repetition that adds no new processing."*

---

### Slide 7: The Core Design Question
- **Avoid beginning with the activity type**.
  - **Weak start**: *"Make a reading worksheet about travel"*
  - **Stronger start**: *"Design an accessible travel reading that recycles past tense verbs and leads to a meaningful speaking task"*
- **Point 1**: The learning purpose controls the AI output.

---

### Slide 8: Decision: Choose the Learning Focus
- **What should learners pay attention to?**
  - **Decide the main focus**: Vocabulary, grammar, fluency, reading skill, listening skill, speaking, or writing.
  - **Decide the role of form**: Is the task for noticing a form or for using language to understand meaning?
- **Point 2**: **Limit the focus** — One clear aim is usually better than several weak aims.

---

### Slide 9: Decision: Define the Learners
- **AI needs constraints, not vague labels**.
  - **Give learner information**: Age, context, approximate level, class size, time, and motivation.
  - **Give language information**: What they already know, what they recently studied, what should be avoided.
  - **Give task conditions**: Pair work, homework, test preparation, reading fluency, discussion, or self-access.
- **Rule**: Do not trust a CEFR label unless the output shows why it fits that level.

---

### Slide 10: Decision: Ask AI to Explain Its Design
- **The explanation is part of quality control**.
  - Ask AI to **state the learning focus**: What should learners attend to?
  - Ask AI to **show recycling**: Where does important language reappear?
  - Ask AI to **justify the level**: What features make the material suitable for these learners?
  - Ask AI to **identify risks**: Unclear instructions, difficult language, factual claims, or cultural assumptions.

---

### Slide 11: A Reusable Prompt Frame
- **Give AI the decisions, not just the topic**:
  - `Create a task sequence for [learners] at [level evidence]`
  - `The learning focus is [......]`
  - `Use [known language] and recycle [... state target items]`
  - `Limit new language to [..... number or type]`
  - `Learners must use the text to [... intended meaningful action]`
  - `Include retrieval, later recycling and a short design explanation`

## Part 2: Material Generation Rules & System Prompt Guidelines

When generating any English Language Teaching (ELT) materials or designing curriculum components (starting with the **Pronunciation Course**), the AI must strictly adhere to the following 8 core rules:

### RULE 1: Sequence & Continuity Over Standalone Tasks
- **Requirement**: Never generate isolated, one-off exercises or standalone worksheets without embedding them into a cohesive learning sequence across time.
- **Execution**: Structure material generation around a multi-stage progression where target items re-emerge naturally across multiple connected texts and tasks.

### RULE 2: Enforce the Four-Fold Balance Principle
All course materials and unit plans must maintain a balanced distribution across four distinct pedagogical functions:
1. **Language-Focused Learning**: Activities that explicitly direct attention to language forms, phonetics, rules, or patterns.
2. **Meaning-Focused Input**: Receptive tasks (listening/reading) where understanding meaning is the primary goal.
3. **Meaning-Focused Output**: Productive tasks (speaking/writing) where learners communicate authentic messages.
4. **Fluency Development**: Speed and automatization drills using familiar, low-demand language.

### RULE 3: Follow the 7-Stage Learning Sequence
Material sets must follow a principled sequence:
`Initial Encounter` ➔ `Supported Processing` ➔ `Repeated Encounter` ➔ `Retrieval` ➔ `Meaningful Use` ➔ `Fluency` ➔ `Later Recycling`

### RULE 4: Purpose-Driven Generation (No Pure Activity Prompts)
- **Requirement**: Never define a generation task by its output format alone (e.g., "create a pronunciation gap-fill").
- **Execution**: Always define the primary **pedagogical aim**, the **target item recycling**, and the **downstream communicative task** before determining the exercise format.

### RULE 5: Strict Attention Control (Focus, Quantity, Quality)
- **Narrow Focus**: Keep learning objectives precise and singular. Avoid overwhelming learners with multiple competing demands in a single task.
- **Sufficient Exposure (Quantity)**: Ensure target items are recycled multiple times across sequences. Never assume single exposure leads to acquisition.
- **Deep Cognitive Processing (Quality)**: Vary encounter contexts. Avoid mindless drills; require learners to notice, retrieve, connect, and apply features meaningfully.

### RULE 6: Explicit Context Constraints & CEFR Level Justification
- **Context Constraints**: Every generation request must specify learner age, context, exact prior knowledge, and specific task constraints.
- **Evidence-Based CEFR**: Never rely on standalone CEFR tags (e.g., "B1"). Output must provide explicit linguistic justification for why the syntax, vocabulary, or phonological demands match the target level.

### RULE 7: Mandatory Self-Explanation & Risk Audit
Every material generated by AI must include a appended **Design Justification & Risk Audit** containing:
1. **Target Learning Focus**: Clear statement of what learners are attending to.
2. **Recycling Trace**: Explicit mapping of where target items re-appear.
3. **Level Verification**: Structural proof of level appropriateness.
4. **Risk Identification**: Audit for unclear instructions, artificial language, cultural bias, or factual errors.

### RULE 8: Standardized Reusable Prompt Template
When prompting or drafting generation specs, use the standard frame:
```text
Create a task sequence for [learner demographic] with evidence of [level details].
- Learning Focus: [Narrow, explicit target focus]
- Known Language & Recycling: [Prior items to recycle]
- New Language Limit: [Max count & item types]
- Meaningful Action: [Authentic task goal]
- Self-Explanation Required: Include retrieval mapping, recycling trace, level justification, and risk audit.
```

## Part 3: Application to the Upcoming Pronunciation Course

When applied specifically to **Pronunciation Course Development**:
1. **Sequence over Isolation**: Rather than isolated minimal pair drills, introduce phonemes/features in meaningful auditory input, provide visual/articulatory support, re-encounter in context, retrieve through discrimination, use in meaningful speaking, and recycle in later units.
2. **Four Strands in Pronunciation**:
   - *Language-focused*: Form-focused ear-training, IPA identification, stress placement, intonation pattern rules.
   - *Meaning-focused input*: Listening to natural dialogues/monologues for comprehension while target sound features are embedded.
   - *Meaning-focused output*: Speaking tasks (e.g., info-gaps, roleplays) where pronunciation clarity directly affects outcome.
   - *Fluency development*: Shadowing, speed reading, or rhythm drills using highly familiar vocabulary.
3. **Narrow Focus**: Focus on specific contrasting features (e.g., /iː/ vs /ɪ/ or final consonant clusters) per unit rather than generic "accent reduction".
