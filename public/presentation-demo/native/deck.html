Hurdle 1: The Habit of Waiting for Instruction

The thought

Who is going to tell me what to do next? Can I just rewrite this dialogue, or do I need someone to sign off on it?

Why it happened

In a traditional job the coordinator hands you the task instructions, the deadlines and the format. Here, that authority simply isn't there, and the first weeks of silence feels like a delay.

The shift

It isn't a delay. If you see something that will help our students learn better, you already have permission. Find a colleague and start.

Heard in week 1

"I sat down on the Monday and waited for a syllabus that was never coming."
05 / 21

Hurdle 2: Wondering If We're Doing Enough

The thought

Did I do enough today? Does anyone know how hard I worked?

Why it happened

Phantom guilt fills the space praise and deadlines used to occupy. Some of us answered messages at midnight to prove our dedication.

The shift

Working late does not make you a hero. Good pronunciation material comes from clear, well-rested minds. Trust that your work speaks for itself.

Heard in week 3

"Nobody asked me for hours, so I kept working to be safe."
06 / 21

Hurdle 3: Holding Back to Be Polite

The thought

Their ideas confuse me — but I don't want to hurt anyone's feelings or sound critical.

Why it happened

Polite silence leaves a teammate alone with a weak lesson — and the confusion lands on a student weeks later.

The shift

Kindness is honest feedback, early: warm about the person, straight about the draft. We critique the work together so the class gets our best.

Heard in week 4

"I noticed the problem on day one. I said it on day nine."

Foundational philosophy 09 / 21

Gabe Newell's Ideology: Why Hierarchies Destroy Creative Value

If you hire smart, creative people and then tell them what to do, you destroy most of their value. You want people who figure out what is valuable and just go do it.

Why pyramids existed

Ronald Coase, 1937: hierarchy was the cheapest way to coordinate repetitive factory work.

Why it breaks creative work

Lesson design is not repetitive. The same layers add friction, delay and distortion instead of order.

Nobody to manage up to

With no supervisor to impress, the whole team's attention points outward — at the learner.

Chet Faliszek
Writer & Project Lead · Left 4 Dead, Portal

Making decisions as equals 17 / 21

How We Make Decisions: The Advice Process

01

You spot the call

Anyone can make any operational decision. Noticing it makes it yours.

02

Ask the experienced

Talk to teammates who know that area — phonetics, recording, assessment.

03

Ask the affected

Talk to whoever will teach, record or mark the thing you are changing. Listen properly.

04

Decide and own it

Make the call, explain your reasons, carry the result.

The one rule

Advice is required. Agreement is not.

Working through differences 18 / 21

When We Disagree:
How We Choose the Best Way

No long debates where people wear each other down. Three pathways, in this order.

Pathway one

The advice call

The person doing the work makes the final call, after hearing the people with experience and the people affected.

Pathway two

The student pilot

If both ideas have merit, run both with a handful of real learners. Student clarity decides, not seniority.

Pathway three

A friendly third peer

If it feels stuck or personal, ask a trusted colleague to sit in on a fifteen-minute coffee chat so everyone is heard.
